Issue history

Budgets, school taxes and spending: how Hanover pays for its schools

How much does Hanover School Division spend, where does the money come from, and what have school taxes cost property owners since 2014? How did provincial funding, tax freezes and rebates shape the board’s choices, and what did trustees decide about reserves, borrowing, banking and their own pay?

Board meetings Jul 2013 – Sept 2026 · 125 decisions in the minutes · Last updated Nov 14, 2026

In short

  • The budget has grown with enrolment. The board approved $85.0 million for 2014/15 and about $146.7 million for 2026/27, debt payments included. Every budget and mill rate the minutes record passed unanimously. The published minutes contain no motion adopting the 2021/22, 2022/23 or 2023/24 budgets.
  • The mill rate fell for years, then rose. Hanover’s school tax rate went from 16.02 mills in 2015 to 11.99 in 2023, partly because property values and new construction grew and partly because the province froze school taxes from 2021 to 2023. Since the freeze ended, the rate has risen to 13.32 in 2026, about $53 more than in 2025 on a home assessed at $354,000.
  • Lean years, then rehiring. To balance its 2023/24 budget the board cut 10.5 teaching positions and did not add the 15 that growth called for. With the freeze lifted, budgets from 2024/25 on added teachers and educational assistants each year, paid for partly by higher school taxes.
  • Thin reserves. The province caps a division’s operating surplus at 4% of spending. Hanover’s fell to 1.9% in June 2023 and stood at 2.9% in June 2026.
  • Borrowing is mostly the province’s. School buildings are financed by loans the board signs with the province, and the province’s grants repay them; the division owed the province about $133.5 million in June 2026. Each year the board also renews a by-law allowing up to $50 million in short-term borrowing on its line of credit.
  • Split votes were about trustee pay, mileage and the bank. Trustees divided on their own pay in 2019 (7–2), 2020 (5–2) and 2021 (6–2, a freeze), on the 2019 mileage policy, and on moving the division’s banking to Scotiabank in 2025 (5–2).

Background: the rules

Two sources of money

A Manitoba school division has two main sources of money for day-to-day costs: grants from the province and a local property tax. In Hanover the province has paid roughly 60% of operating revenue; the board’s 2016 budget release put it at approximately 60%, with the other 40% from school taxes.

Each year the board works out how much it needs beyond the province’s grants. That amount is the special requirement, and the tax that raises it is the special levy. Under the Public Schools Act the board must send each municipality its share on or before March 15, and the municipality must impose a tax sufficient to raise it. The school tax line on a property tax bill in Blumenort, Grunthal, Landmark, Mitchell, Niverville, Steinbach and the other Hanover communities is this levy. The province also charges its own Education Support Levy on property other than farm and residential property; the board does not set it.

What a mill rate is

The levy is spread over all taxable property in the division as a mill rate: one mill is $1 of tax for every $1,000 of a property’s taxable assessment. When property values rise at a reassessment, or new homes and businesses are built, the same levy can be raised with a lower mill rate. A lower mill rate therefore does not always mean a lower tax bill. In 2016, for example, the board cut the mill rate from 16.02 to 14.94, but because of reassessment the levy still rose 2.24%.

How a budget is approved

Before approving its budget, a board must consult its parent councils and present the proposed budget at an open meeting, with public notice. The province is supposed to announce each division’s grants by February 15, which leaves boards about a month to set the levy. Hanover’s budget motions list an operating budget (salaries, buses, heat and everything else that runs schools), transfers to capital (operating money set aside for buses and equipment) and debt servicing (payments on building loans, which the province reimburses).

In 2015 the education minister wrote that school divisions could no longer keep a surplus above 4%. Hanover has since measured its reserve against that 4% line.

By-laws and trustee pay

Some decisions must be made by by-law, which takes three readings, usually at two meetings. Borrowing is one, and trustee pay is another. Under Policy BE, trustees receive an annual indemnity (their pay), which “shall be reviewed annually and shall be increased by the Consumer Price Index (CPI) for Winnipeg for the previous calendar year”. The board sets the amount each year in a Trustee Indemnity by-law.

2014–2016: Growth, reassessments and the 4% surplus cap

Budgets that grew with enrolment

The board approved the 2014/15 budget 7–0 at $85,032,900, with a mill rate of 15.59, 7.1% lower than in 2013. Finance committee papers that spring show a draft budget built both with and without a $500,000 surplus from 2013/14. The next year the rate rose 2.7% to 16.02, the highest of the period, in a budget approved 8–0.

Before the 2016/17 budget, Steinbach City Council asked the division to hold the line. Council voted 6–1 to ask HSD to “implement a zero tax increase this year”. The board cut the mill rate to 14.94, but the levy itself rose 2.24% because of reassessment. Its release said the budget would pay for more teachers, learning coaches and resource staff, and for bus cameras and GPS tracking.

“Spreading the required cost over time, evenly and marginally, reflects a recognition of household budget pressures.”

Rick Peters, board finance chair, in the division’s budget release · Mar 1, 2016

The same release said HSD had “the lowest cost-per-student average in the province of Manitoba”, a claim the division repeated in later budget releases.

Public consultations

The board held its public budget consultation at a parent advisory council (PAC) meeting each fall or winter: in February 2015 (a letter to PAC chairs is listed that month), on Nov 17, 2015, and in late October in 2016 and 2017, according to the committee papers listed in the minutes. The minutes do not record who spoke or what was said.

A cap on surpluses

In February 2015 the board’s finance committee reported a letter from the education minister: divisions could “no longer be able maintain a surplus in excess of 4%”. That ceiling comes back in almost every later budget debate.

2017–2020: Small provincial increases and a 2% tax limit

Funding falls behind growth

After the Progressive Conservatives took office in 2016, provincial increases shrank. In February 2017 Steinbach Online reported that Hanover’s 2.3% increase was “the lowest increase since the 1990’s”. The board approved the 2017/18 budget 7–0 on condition that the province keep funding small kindergarten-to-Grade-3 classes (the “20 K–3” initiative). The mill rate rose 2.41% to 15.29. A week later the province confirmed it would still provide the $960,000, and the budget stood.

From 2018 the province asked boards to limit any increases to their local education property tax to two per cent. Hanover’s levy rose 2.72% in 2018, but growth in assessment meant the mill rate fell to 15.14 and the division estimated about $17 less on a $250,000 house.

2019: spending cuts

For 2019/20 the province raised school funding by $6.6 million in total, about 0.5%, and Hanover received 1.6%. The province’s release asked divisions to cap increases to their local education property tax at two per cent and announced a review of kindergarten-to-Grade-12 education. The board approved a $101.9 million budget 7–0 and a mill rate of 15.17, up 0.15%.

“…our operational needs were closer to $3 million so simple math says we have to reduce spending.”

Rick Peters, finance committee chair, to Steinbach Online · Mar 6, 2019

Steinbach Online reported that the cuts came mainly from transportation and maintenance and that the board drew an additional $320,000 of surplus funds. Later that spring the division said it could no longer afford an Addictions Foundation of Manitoba counsellor, whose work passed to school guidance staff, and the board cut staff appreciation funding from $27,000 to $7,000. When the 2018/19 statements came in, Steinbach Online reported a $3.4 million accumulated surplus; finance chair Ron Falk said “everybody was under budget.”

2020: asking the public, and hiring support staff

For 2020/21 the board moved its consultation online. More than 1,200 people answered a Thoughtexchange survey (1,246, according to Steinbach Online), and the board said that in response it prioritized front-line support services: 15 educational assistants, five guidance counsellors, 3.5 learning support teachers and a social worker, paid for by reallocating money and using surplus. The $104.6 million budget passed 7–0, and the mill rate fell to 14.64.

“There are students that are coming with a lot of baggage to school and our teachers are, quite frankly, burning out. They need help.”

Ron Falk, finance chair, to Steinbach Online · Feb 5, 2020

The division’s release said that “for the third consecutive year” there would be no increase on the average homeowner’s school tax bill. (In 2019 the mill rate had risen 0.15%; Steinbach Online put the effect at about $2 on a $250,000 home.) Schools closed to in-person classes weeks later because of COVID-19; the pandemic’s effect on the budget is covered in COVID-19.

2021–2023: The tax freeze, rebates, Bill 64 and cuts

The province freezes school taxes

In February 2021 the province announced that “school divisions will freeze their education property taxes”, with a Property Tax Offset Grant to make up the difference. Hanover’s funding rose to about $57.6 million, up 3.2%; finance chair Rick Peters told the Carillon it was a fair number. The board set the 2021 mill rate at 14.25, down 2.67%, by 8–0. In April the province’s budget began rebating part of the school tax directly to owners: 25% in 2021, rising to 50% in 2023, toward the “eventual elimination” of school taxes on property.

Steinbach Online reported a 2021/22 budget of $96,751,300 that cut about four educator positions. Peters said, “Anytime you touch the classroom it is a difficult decision to make.” The published minutes for 2021 do not include a motion adopting that budget.

Bill 64

In March 2021 the government introduced Bill 64, the Education Modernization Act, which would have replaced the elected English-language school boards with a provincial education authority and, as CBC reported, continued “gradually eliminating education property taxes”. Under that plan school boards would no longer set a local levy. The board wrote to the community that it “strongly opposes eliminating locally elected school boards”. In September 2021 interim Premier Kelvin Goertzen announced he would scrap the controversial bill. The governance side of Bill 64 is covered in Board governance.

2022: fewer students, fewer teachers

The province kept the freeze for a second year: “education property taxes will remain frozen.” Enrolment had dropped during the pandemic. Steinbach Online reported that the balanced $99.7 million budget cut 16 teaching positions and two new buses; finance chair Rick Peters said, “With less students, it means less teachers in the division.” The board set the 2022 mill rate at 13.74 by 8–0.

2023: “fiscally challenging times”

The province announced a 6.1% increase in school operating funding for 2023/24. Finance chair Danielle Funk told the Carillon it was less than it seemed:

“While it sounds great and it seems really exciting at first, it turns out to be not quite as much as it first appeared,”

Danielle Funk, finance committee chair, to the Carillon · Feb 13, 2023

About 50 people came to the public budget meeting in March, the Carillon reported, where Superintendent Shelley Amos said, “The amount funded to us is not as astronomical as the government makes it sound,” according to the paper. Board chair Ron Falk told Steinbach Online the cuts would be “somewhat painful, very painful.” The board set the 2023 mill rate at 11.99, down 12.74%, by 9–0.

The division’s budget release of Mar 23, 2023 set out the gap. Provincial operating funding rose $1,123,800 (1.8%), plus $850,800 in offset grant, while costs were projected to rise $4,668,000, mostly from inflation and expected wage settlements, leaving a $2,693,400 shortfall. To close it the board cut 10.5 teaching positions ($1,250,300), bus replacements ($314,600), maintenance projects ($292,000), IT infrastructure ($100,000) and $236,500 in divisional programs, and used $500,000 of surplus. It also did not hire the 15 teachers needed for an expected 279 more students. The Carillon reported that the Grade 2 swim program was among the cuts.

“Hanover School Division adheres to the taxation guidelines provided by the Province of Manitoba, which no longer allows divisions to raise additional funds through taxation.”

Board of Trustees, 2023/24 budget release · Mar 23, 2023

“This isn’t a division who throws money at insignificant things.”

Danielle Funk, finance committee chair, to the Carillon · Mar 30, 2023

In May the board received letters from Education Minister Wayne Ewasko about the Property Tax Offset Grant and funding; the minutes list them without their contents. Superintendent Amos told Steinbach Online that the offset grant “hasn’t kept pace with what we would actually receive” from local taxes. The 2022/23 year still ended with an operating deficit of $1,092,930, and the reserve fell to 1.9% of spending, less than half the 4% ceiling.

2024–2026: Taxes rise again and staff are added

The freeze ends

The NDP government elected in October 2023 raised school operating funding 3.4% for 2024/25 and, as CBC reported, said school divisions would not be penalized for raising taxes. Hanover’s provincial funding rose $4,026,249, or 6.2%. At the February 2024 consultation, Hanover Teachers’ Association president Kevin Martens and others raised class sizes; Steinbach Online reported that the previous year’s cuts had meant a rise in the teacher-student ratio by two students. Finance chair Ron Falk:

“I think it’s safe to say though that not having a tax increase is not going to work,”

Ron Falk, finance committee chair, to Steinbach Online · Feb 8, 2024

On Mar 5, 2024 the board approved the 2024 mill rate of 12.74 and the 2024/25 budget, each 9–0. The levy rose 6.25%, raising an additional $3.7 million, and the budget paid for 16.5 more teachers for about 398 more students. The division called it “the first school tax increase in the Hanover School Division since 2017.”

Rebates become a tax credit

The province’s 2024 budget replaced the school tax rebate on homes with a flat credit. Starting in 2025, homeowners get the Homeowners Affordability Tax Credit (HATC) of up to $1,500 on principal residences, while farm property kept its 50% rebate. The credit has since risen to $1,600 and is set to reach $1,700. The province pays the credit and rebates to the division, so they do not change how much the school budget raises. In the 2026/27 budget the board’s special requirement is $56,693,324, of which the province covers $14,930,435 through the HATC, $2,611,775 through the farm school tax rebate and $3,041,599 through offset grants. The figure was $46,432,019 in 2024/25 and $50,195,184 in 2025/26.

Because of the credit, what each owner pays now depends on the home’s value as well as the mill rate. In 2025 the division estimated that a home assessed at $354,000 would see no change in school taxes, while owners of more valuable homes would pay more and owners of less valuable ones less.

Lost federal funding and a deficit warning

In November 2024 the division gave approximately 93 educational assistants layoff notices after an unexpected loss of federal funding under Jordan’s Principle, a federal program for First Nations children. In February 2025 the division projected a $1.1 million deficit for 2024/25. A year earlier the board chair had forecast an $800,000 deficit for 2023/24. Both years ended in surplus: $704,989 in 2023/24 and $923,348 in 2024/25, which lifted the reserve to 3.1%.

2025/26 and 2026/27 budgets

For 2025/26 Hanover’s provincial funding rose 2.2%; Steinbach Online noted that “the provincial average is 3.4 per cent.” The Carillon reported that the province had eliminated the enrolment change grant. The board approved a $136.6 million budget 7–0 with 12 more teachers and 14 educational assistants, and a mill rate of 12.36, 3% lower than in 2024 after reassessment. The published minutes record no separate motion setting the 2025 or 2026 mill rate; the rates come from the division’s releases.

About 25 people came to the public consultation for 2026/27: Steinbach Online counted roughly 25 parents, community members and staff on Oct 28, 2025. On Mar 3, 2026 the board approved the 2026/27 budget 7–0: 28 more teaching positions, six of them literacy and numeracy support teachers, 22 educational assistants and $500,000 for Grade 1 literacy. The mill rate rose 7.97% to 13.32. The division said its provincial funding rose 4.1%. The budget plans to use a portion of its surplus, a planned operating deficit of $250,000. Board chair Dallas Wiebe, in an October 2026 letter to the Carillon, wrote that the board had “kept the mill-rate increase below eight percent” while neighbouring divisions raised theirs more.

2013–2026: Borrowing, audits, banking, expenses and trustee pay

Borrowing: by-laws and promissory notes

Most of the board’s borrowing votes are paperwork for projects the province has already approved. Until 2020 the board gave first reading to debenture by-laws listing each project’s cost, from roof replacements to the new Niverville high school. A debenture is a long-term loan; the division’s are twenty-year loans whose principal and interest are “funded entirely by grants from the Province of Manitoba, except for the debt on self-funded capital projects”. From 2021 the board instead approves promissory notes to the Province of Manitoba, a written promise to repay a provincial loan, such as $8,373,500 for modular classrooms in March 2021 and $12,022,100 in January 2025 for projects including the new K–4 school. The division’s debt to the province rose from $103.7 million in June 2023 to $133.5 million in June 2026 as new schools were built. Which schools were built and why is covered in Growing enrolment.

Separately, each June the board passes a temporary borrowing by-law setting a limit on short-term borrowing on its line of credit for the coming year; the audited statements say the division’s overdrafts “are secured by temporary borrowing by-laws”. The limit rose from $39 million for 2014/15 to $50 million from 2023/24. Every borrowing vote in the minutes was unanimous.

Audits and banking

The accounting firm MNP has audited the division throughout the period. The board extended its contract for two years in 2017, 2019 and 2021, and for three years, through 2025/26, in 2024; each vote was unanimous. The board accepted the audited statements by public motion each fall through 2022. For 2022/23 to 2025/26 the minutes contain no such motion, although the statements are posted on hsd.ca. The finance committee also reviewed the superintendent’s and secretary-treasurer’s Visa and expense claims each quarter, as the committee papers listed in the minutes show.

Steinbach Credit Union held the division’s banking, with two-year extensions approved in 2015 and 2016 (to June 30, 2019). A banking tender came to the finance committee in February 2019 and was “to be brought forward to the March 6, 2019 Board meeting”; we found no later motion on it until 2025. Updates on “SCU Banking / CAFT Processing” appear in the committee papers from October 2024 to May 2025; the minutes do not describe them. On Jun 26, 2025 the board accepted Scotiabank’s proposal for banking services for five years, 5–2 with one abstention. The minutes give no reasons, and we found no news coverage. The June 2026 statements show a line of credit with Scotiabank of $30,000,000.

Credit cards and mileage, 2019

In the fall of 2019 the board adopted two expense policies. Policy DC, Use of Divisional Credit Cards, passed both votes unanimously (first reading Oct 1, final reading Nov 5, 9–0); it limits cards to authorized division business and says misuse can lead to discipline up to dismissal. The split votes that night were on Policy DE, Mileage Reimbursement, which sets the mileage rate at “one ($0.01) cent less than the Manitoba School Boards Association mileage rate”. A motion to remove that one-cent clause was defeated 4–5, and the policy then passed 7–2. The minutes do not record the debate. (The board’s motion list files both mileage votes under the credit-card item; the minutes show they were on Policy DE.)

Trustee pay

Trustees set their own pay by by-law each year. Steinbach Online, using figures from Secretary-Treasurer Kevin Heide, reported 2019 indemnities of $14,421 for the chair, $12,203 for the vice-chair and $11,093 for other trustees, up from $14,083, $11,917 and $10,833 in 2018. Trustees had just lost a federal tax break on one-third of their earnings; the board “elected not to raise their yearly indemnity despite the loss of a federal tax break” beyond the usual inflation adjustment, and the by-law passed 7–2. Rick Peters told Steinbach Online, “We decided just to pay our taxes and take that cut.”

In 2020 a 2.3% inflation increase passed 5–2, taking a trustee to $11,348 and, according to Steinbach Online, the chair to $14,753 and the vice-chair to $12,484. Trustee Shannon Friesen, who voted against, said: “For myself, knowing that all of our staff are on a wage freeze, I opposed it on a principle.” In 2021 the board voted 6–2 for a 0% increase, “foregoing a CPI index increase”; Steinbach Online reported the board acted in solidarity with staff whose wages were frozen, and that it voted six to two against the raise.

Later increases passed with no votes against: 3.1% for 2022, 3.3% for 2023 and 3.0% for 2024 (both approved in April 2024), 1.2% for 2025 and 2.7% for 2026. Ron Falk explained that Winnipeg’s CPI for 2023 had been 7.7%, “obviously too high for us to live with,” so trustees matched the 3.3% employees received; the board also raised the daily indemnity for extra meetings by $50, to a special rate of $150. “Trustees determine their own indemnities, just like MLA’s and city councillors,” he said. For calendar 2025 the division’s audited disclosure reports $130,213.03 in total compensation for all trustees; the individual amounts, listed by name, were between $13,485.65 and $18,071.55 for eight of the nine trustees.

What the new board inherits

The board elected on Oct 28, 2026 (Jenn Elliot, Jeff Friesen, Shayne Barkman, Jacqueline Morgan, Tess Doerksen, Josh Loewen, Jeremy Neufeld, Erin Williams and Cheryl Froese) took office in November 2026. From the record:

  • The 2027/28 budget. The board must hold a public budget meeting before it approves the budget, and send its levy to municipalities by Mar 15, 2027.
  • A reserve below the ceiling. The reserve was $3,534,251, or 2.9% of spending, on June 30, 2026. The 2026/27 budget plans to draw $250,000 from it.
  • Contracts. The MNP audit extension covered the years through 2025/26; the minutes show no appointment yet for 2026/27. The Scotiabank banking agreement runs five years from 2025/26.
  • Borrowing. The board approved a $50 million temporary borrowing limit for 2026/27, and promissory notes for current projects keep coming to the board.
  • Trustee pay. Under Policy BE the 2027 indemnity by-law will start from Winnipeg’s CPI for 2026.
  • Provincial changes. The province has said the HATC will rise to $1,700. As of 2025 CBC reported no ceiling on the special levy boards can set.

About this page

Built from the board’s published minutes (Jul 2013 – Nov 2026), agendas, budget documents, audited financial statements, policies and news releases on hsd.ca, and from news coverage and provincial sources linked in the text. See How these reports are made and Data accuracy.

What we searched, and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Searched: all 1,660 motions for budget, mill rate, levy, tax, borrowing, debenture, promissory note, non-resident fees, audit, bank, credit card, mileage, indemnity and remuneration; the full minutes and agendas for the same terms and for funding, surplus, deficit, reserve and consultation; the three posted budget documents (2024/25 to 2026/27), the four posted audited statements (2022/23 to 2025/26) and the 2025 compensation disclosure; Policies BE, DC and DE; HSD’s budget news releases; Steinbach Online, the Carillon, CBC, Manitoba government releases and the Public Schools Act.
  • What the table includes: budgets, mill rates, borrowing by-laws and promissory notes, non-resident fees, audited statements, audit and banking contracts, the 2019 credit-card and mileage policies, and trustee pay. We left out routine monthly cheque-register approvals, bank signing-officer changes after each chair election, and bus and construction tenders, which are covered in other reports.
  • The record’s limits: HSD publishes no meeting video. The minutes list the finance committee’s papers (budget summaries, variance reports, consultation feedback) by title only. Votes are counts. Closed Committee of the Whole minutes are not published.
  • Couldn’t confirm: how or when the 2021/22, 2022/23 and 2023/24 budgets were adopted (no motion appears in the published minutes); motions setting the 2025 and 2026 mill rates; motions accepting the audited statements after 2021/22; first readings of most indemnity by-laws; indemnity amounts after 2020 (only percentages appear in the minutes); the reasons for the split votes on mileage and banking; what came of the 2019 banking tender; non-resident fees for 2021/22; the Carillon’s 2021 article on the tax freeze and HSD’s mill rate, and other articles listed in the minutes that we could not open.
  • Where sources differ: Steinbach Online put the 2019 mill rate at 15.16 in one article; the minutes say 15.17. Provincial funding increases reported at the funding announcement sometimes differ from later figures: for 2017, 2.3% (Steinbach Online) against 2.6% in the board’s release; for 2021, 3.2% at the announcement against 2.39% when the budget passed; for 2026, 4.1% in the board’s release against 3.0% in the province’s backgrounder. The table uses the board’s release where there is one. The motion accepting the 2020/21 statements says “June 30, 2020”.
