COVID-19 in Hanover schools, 2020–2022: who decided what

When COVID-19 closed Manitoba classrooms in March 2020, who made the rules for Hanover schools: the province, public health or the school board? What did the board itself decide, how did families and staff fare, and what is left of it today?

Board meetings Jul 2013 – Sept 2026 · 28 decisions in the minutes · Last updated Nov 14, 2026

In short

  • The province made the big calls. Closing classrooms, reopening them, masks, remote learning and the staff vaccination-or-testing rule were provincial and public health orders. In August 2020 Interim Superintendent Shelley Amos said, “we do not make the decisions here.”
  • The division decided how to carry them out. In April 2020 it laid off about 85% of its educational assistants, opened free school WiFi, and in June 2020 reopened classrooms a week later than the province allowed. When about 740 fewer students enrolled than it had budgeted for that fall, it said it was not reducing staff.
  • Hanover was hit hard in November 2020. With 40% of COVID-19 tests in Steinbach coming back positive, the province’s chief public health officer moved 19 Hanover schools to remote learning from Nov 24, 2020, while classrooms elsewhere in Manitoba stayed open.
  • The board’s own votes were few. It met by video and e-mail, live-streamed one meeting, paid bus drivers for extra cleaning, and pushed back religious-exercise petition deadlines and school tours. Every recorded vote on this topic from 2020 to 2022 was unanimous.
  • Mandates brought protests in 2021 and 2022. Parents rallied at the division office in August 2021, and on Feb 10, 2022 a protest against mandates at Steinbach Regional Secondary School (SRSS) led to a Hold and Secure. The board said protest on school property “is not an appropriate place.”
  • One policy lasts. The board revised its Communicable Diseases policy in 2020 and reviewed it again in 2024 and 2025, when it passed 4–2 at first reading. The minutes do not say what changed.

Background: the rules

During the pandemic the important rules for schools came from two places. Manitoba’s chief provincial public health officer, Dr. Brent Roussin, issued public health orders, and the Department of Education set the plans schools had to follow: when classrooms opened, who wore masks, and when a school or region moved to remote learning. From fall 2020 the province used a colour-coded system, the #RestartMB Pandemic Response System; at its highest level, Critical (red), most students learned from home.

School boards could not set those rules aside. What a board and its staff controlled was how a division carried them out: staffing, buses, technology, timing within the province’s windows, and its own meetings. Hanover’s standing rule is Policy CB, Communicable Diseases, first written in 2006. It says the division will “work collaboratively with local and provincial health authorities,” and that a principal may, after consulting the public health nurse, require a sick student to stay home.

Two meeting terms come up below. In camera means a closed meeting of all trustees (the Committee of the Whole); its minutes are adopted later in a public meeting but are not published. An e-mail meeting is a closed meeting held by e-mail. Hanover used e-mail meetings long before COVID-19; one appears in the minutes as early as July 2013.

Spring 2020: Classrooms close and the board goes remote

The province suspends classes

On Mar 13, 2020 the province announced that public health officials had advised suspending classes in Manitoba K–12 schools from Mar 23. Hanover said it would remain in session until March 23, with desks spaced apart and all extra-curricular activities cancelled. Parents could keep children home, and many did: attendance dropped by 30% on Mar 16. On Mar 31 the province suspended classroom learning indefinitely. Superintendent Randy Dueck told Steinbach Online, “Our only option is to move forward and make the best of this that we possibly can.”

Learning from home

One early worry was internet access at home. The division ran a free, filtered drive-up WiFi pilot at Landmark Collegiate, and by mid-April 4,300 Chromebooks had been handed out to high school students. Within days it added free WiFi at schools in Blumenort, Grunthal and Mitchell. Trustees received a report on online learning resources during COVID-19; its contents are not in the minutes.

Educational assistants laid off

With classrooms empty, the province asked divisions to reassign support staff where possible, but said it would be up to the divisions whether they have to lay off people. In mid-April Hanover announced it would lay off 85% of its educational assistants (EAs), the staff who support students with special needs; 33 stayed on. Board chair Rick Peters said, “It was a tough decision to make, but that is what we have done.” No motion on the layoffs appears in the public minutes; the board met only in closed session that month.

The EAs’ union, the Christian Labour Association of Canada (CLAC), disagreed. Regional director Geoff Dueck Thiessen said “we wanted our members to be kept on the payroll,” and CBC reported the union’s estimate that about 33 of roughly 220 EAs would remain. When some students returned in June, EAs stayed home, and the division laid off an additional four. Dueck said that if school resumed in September, “we will want all of our EAs all back…”

How the board met

The board held no public meeting between Mar 3 and May 5, 2020. Its closed meetings went on: by e-mail on Mar 12 and Mar 26, and “remotely due to COVID-19” on Apr 7 and Apr 21. What was decided there is not public. The May 5 public meeting was itself a “remote regular meeting,” where trustees heard a COVID-19 update on “both provincial direction and divisional direction”. For the Jun 2 meeting the boardroom was closed to visitors, and HSD said it would be broadcasting the meeting as a live event on our YouTube Channel. That is the only live-streamed meeting we found; HSD publishes no meeting video today.

Two other board decisions that spring were shaped by the pandemic. On Mar 3, just before the closures, the board gave first reading to an amended Policy CB, Communicable Diseases, and adopted it 9–0 on May 5. On Jun 2 it extended the deadline for parent petitions for religious exercises and instruction to July 31, 2020 “due to the COVID-19 pandemic”; that story is told in Religion in schools.

A week later than the province

The province allowed schools to bring students back in small groups from Jun 1. Hanover waited a week: students had to “wait for one additional week”, with visits by appointment, no buses, and attendance “optional and at the discretion of parents.” Graduates got lawn signs and drive-in convocations instead of ceremonies; see Learning and programs. The year also left the division with unspent money: the board’s August package lists a Carillon story headlined “Pandemic savings for HSD come to $2.2 million”. The budget side is covered in Budget and taxes.

2020–2021: Back in class, then “Code Red”

Restart rules came from the province

Students returned full-time in September 2020 under a provincial plan. On Aug 19 the province ruled that masks will be required for students in grades 4 to 12, staff and visitors where two metres of distance wasn’t possible, and on Sept 1 it extended masks to all school buses, including kindergarten to Grade 3. Shelley Amos, who had been named interim superintendent following the retirement of Randy Dueck, said the division received many complaints about masks: “We respect everybody’s right to their opinion but I do want our public to know that we do not make the decisions here.” Steinbach Online added that the division has absolutely no say on what is or is not mandated.

The board’s own adjustments were small. It postponed school tours and presentations until January 2021 “to reduce visitors in our school and respect the guidelines of social distancing in the board room”; no school presentation appears in the minutes again until a video presentation in October 2021. The by-laws allowing religious programs said they would start “in such a manner that logistical requirements related to the pandemic can be addressed.” Trustees began joining meetings remotely, marked “(Remote)” in the roll call from August 2020. Finance reports on COVID-related expenses went to trustees but are not in the minutes.

Families who stayed home

Many families did not come back. Assistant Superintendent Geri Harder-Robson said home-schooling in Hanover had essentially doubled, to about 500 families. Reasons varied. Some worried about the virus; others objected to the rules. One parent, L.H., told Steinbach Online, “If masks would still be highly recommended rather than mandatory, I would have sent my kids to school.” For around 60 students who had weak immune systems, or lived with someone who did, the division offered remote learning with a medical note.

Enrolment fell well short of the budget. Amos said the division had budgeted for about 8,476 students but “as of September 30th, we were at 7,738.” She said, “We are not reducing our staff.” Enrolment recovered by 2022; see Schools and growth.

First cases, and pay for bus drivers

The first known case in a Hanover school was reported at Kleefeld School in October 2020; public health did not believe it had spread at school. Bus drivers had extra cleaning to do. On Nov 3 the board voted 9–0 to pay drivers “COVID pay for additional cleaning protocols”: half an hour for two cleanings a day, three-quarters of an hour for three to five, and an hour for six or more, back-dated to the start of the school year and lasting until cleaning returned to pre-COVID levels.

Hanover alone goes to red

By mid-November Steinbach Online was reporting test-positivity rates of 40% in Steinbach and 30% in Hanover. The province had moved all of Manitoba to red on Nov 12, but with no change to kindergarten to Grade 12 schooling. On Nov 20 it announced that the Hanover School Division and surrounding schools will move to Critical (red) from Nov 24: 19 Hanover schools in all. The release stressed that the move was “not a reflection of school-based virus transmission.”

Students moved to teacher-led remote learning at home, though some, such as young children of critical-services workers, could still attend. Global News reported that 950 of 7,800 students qualified to stay in class. Amos said the division was better prepared than in spring, when “it was more of a crisis mode,” and that parents were divided:

“Some have wanted this to happen much earlier, and others have felt like they want schools to remain open. We have two camps and everything in between.”

Shelley Amos, interim superintendent, to CBC News · Nov 24, 2020

On the day before the switch, board chair Ron Falk called it “a bit of a transition day for sure, trying to get everything lined up.” The board’s own Dec 1 meeting was held almost entirely online, with seven of the nine trustees marked “(Virtual)”, and it met again by e-mail on Dec 18. Hanover rejoined the rest of the province in January. Education Minister Kelvin Goertzen said Hanover would “go back in line with the rest of the province after the Christmas break,” when grades 7 to 12 everywhere learned remotely for two weeks.

Spring 2021: staying in class

In the third wave of May 2021, schools in Winnipeg and Brandon went remote, but in-class learning continued in Hanover, with extra-curricular activities suspended and no indoor singing. Asked what students had lost, Assistant Superintendent Leanne Peters reported from the division’s student advisory council: “One of the most significant things was friendships and relationships,” and learning had become “way more surface-level.”

2021–2022: Vaccine rules, testing and Omicron

Masks and the staff vaccination rule

On Aug 24, 2021 the province required school staff to be vaccinated or tested regularly; the first dose was due by Sept 7, the first day of school. Masks stayed mandatory indoors. Amos stressed that “it is not Hanover that has decided that masks will be mandatory and vaccines required for staff,” adding, “The last thing we want is to have our schools shut down again…”

On Aug 30, parents, grandparents and children filled the front lawn of the Hanover School Division head office to protest the mask and vaccine mandates. Falk said, “People are certainly free to protest and gather and express their views.” The protest was not on the agenda of the board meeting the next evening, and the Aug 31 minutes do not mention it.

A parent’s presentation to the board

At a special meeting on Sept 7, 2021, a parent, S.F., presented her position on COVID procedures and mask mandates in the division. According to Steinbach Online, she had organized protests the week before, brought petitions against the mandates and against vaccination clinics in schools, and said she would call for the trustees and the interim superintendent to resign if her questions were not answered. “Why would there be threats when we could have just a conversation,” Trustee Brad Unger asked.

The board answered publicly three days later. Falk said trustees “would be following the public health orders” and that, as the board understood it, trustees have no control over mask mandates or vaccines. He was responding to a reported comment from the education minister’s office that divisions could make their own choices: “That is a troubling statement and if that in fact is the reality then we have much bigger questions and concerns.” While some divisions added their own staff mandates, Hanover continued to rely on the province. The board also held a closed e-mail meeting on Sept 2, 2021; its subject is not public.

Outside the minutes

In September 2021 Dr. Roussin said nearly half of the daily cases were coming from Southern Health–Santé Sud, a region with 15% of the province’s people. The Winnipeg Free Press described the Hanover health district as one in which less than 50 per cent of eligible residents are double-vaccinated, and reported 29 cases in Hanover schools in the 14 days to Oct 26. A teacher quoted anonymously in that story said the division was acting as if the situation were normal. The board’s package also lists a Carillon story headlined “Trustees ignore vaccination survey”, which we could not open.

Testing staff who weren’t vaccinated

The province’s testing order took effect Oct 18, requiring rapid testing of front-line public servants who were not fully vaccinated. Hanover set up weekly testing at its administration office, and reported that 85% of 1,100 employees had shown proof of vaccination, leaving about 165 to be tested. Falk told the Free Press that upwards of 100 staff members were in the testing program, and by November the division reported a decline in the number of employees who require regular testing. In December the board said it was confident in the ventilation systems in all its schools, and in early 2022 trustees received a copy of its application to the province’s school ventilation fund.

Omicron, January 2022

As Omicron spread over the holidays, the Manitoba Teachers’ Society president, James Bedford, was calling on the province to move all schools to Code Red. The province instead delayed the return to Jan 10, then shifted to a one-week remote learning period for most Manitoba students. When classes resumed, Hanover had enough rapid tests to offer five to each student in kindergarten to Grade 6. Amos said closing a class, cohort or school would be the “last resort.” Four of the eight trustees present, along with Amos and Leanne Peters, joined the Feb 1, 2022 meeting remotely, the last time the minutes record remote attendance.

Spring 2022: The SRSS protest and the end of the mandates

Feb 10, 2022 at Steinbach Regional Secondary

On the morning of Feb 10, parents opposed to the mandates organized a protest and student walkout outside SRSS. Steinbach Online counted somewhere between 100 and 150 students leaving a school of around 1,700; about a third of the crowd outside were adults. RCMP said that at its busiest point there were between 100 and 200 people. At 11 a.m. the school went into a Hold and Secure, with doors locked; Steinbach Online reported this was due to demonstrators attempting to gain unauthorized entry into the school. The division said “Protestors were on school property and made efforts to enter the school building,” and that some adults engaged in banging on windows and doors with fists and flagpoles. RCMP said no arrests were made and no tickets were handed out. That evening the division announced SRSS would shift to remote learning the next day.

Premier Heather Stefanson and Education Minister Wayne Ewasko said “protesting at a school is not an appropriate place.” Steinbach Online reported that many parents and students expressed anger and frustration at the protest; one student who walked out told Steinbach Online it was partly to support a school security guard let go over the vaccination-or-testing rule. When classes resumed, the SRSS principals wrote that the school would approach the new semester “with optimism, tolerance, and kindness regardless of political or personal opinions.” The protest also comes up in Safety and discipline.

The board’s statement

A week later the board thanked SRSS staff and senior administration and backed the decision to lock the doors:

“Protesting is a fundamental right in Canada and an essential part of our democracy. However, protesting on school or daycare property is not an appropriate place. It was disheartening and disappointing to learn how this protest disrupted the school day and instilled fear in some of our students.”

Board of Trustees, statement regarding school protest · Feb 17, 2022

The minutes record no motion on the statement; the board’s March package lists the news coverage, including a Carillon story headlined “School flag stolen during protest”. In April it listed a Carillon story headlined “Internal documents detail verbal, physical assault during SRSS protest”; we could not open it.

Mask exemptions

Students could be excused from wearing a mask. The Free Press reported that Hanover had approved at least 105 requests among its student population of 7,760, nearly double the number in a Winnipeg division more than twice its size, and that SRSS had the most in the division: 23. Amos was not available for an interview and Falk did not respond, the paper said; in an e-mailed statement Amos said the department does not require a doctor’s note.

The mandates end

On Feb 11 the province announced that “Mask requirements and other restrictions will be lifted effective March 15.” Amos said Hanover did not get a heads up before it was made. Proof of vaccination was no longer required for school staff from Mar 1, and from Mar 15 HSD students and staff could choose whether to wear masks indoors. Amos noted that the school division has always followed public health orders. By then she had been named superintendent permanently; the board’s package lists the Carillon story on her appointment.

After 2022: What lasted

Recovery

In April 2022 the province asked divisions to report on the pandemic’s effects on students, to develop a long-term provincial recovery learning response plan, and added money for students with special needs. The published minutes do not include Hanover’s report. Pandemic rules are not mentioned in the minutes after mid-2022; the last COVID item is a 2023 Free Press story on a proposed lawsuit over COVID restrictions, listed without comment.

Remote learning as a tool

The habit of learning from home outlived the pandemic. In March 2021 the board voted that Niverville schools would provide remote learning during the week of the Manitoba Winter Games rather than move spring break. After a record nine days of weather closures in the winter of 2021/22, Steinbach Online reported that virtual learning might be offered on cold days.

The Communicable Diseases policy

Policy CB came back twice. In 2024 the board gave first reading to an amended version 8–0 on May 7 and passed it 7–1 on Jun 27. In 2025 it gave first reading 4–2 on May 6 and final reading 6–0 on Jun 3. The minutes don’t say what changed or why some trustees voted against. The version on hsd.ca says it was last reviewed June 27, 2024. It says parents who refuse or neglect to get treatment for a child with a communicable disease commit an offence under the Public Schools Act, and that a student under a health order must stay home from school.

Where trustees disagreed

The minutes record how many trustees voted for and against, not who, except when a trustee asks for a recorded vote. Every vote on this topic from 2020 to 2022 was unanimous. The non-unanimous votes came later, on the Communicable Diseases policy:

In public, the disagreement on this topic came from outside the board: from residents who asked trustees to set the mandates aside, and from teachers and others who wanted stronger measures. The board’s position, stated by Falk in 2021, was that it would follow the public health orders. Many pandemic matters were handled in closed meetings, including e-mail meetings, whose minutes are not published, so other discussions may not be in the record.

What the new board inherits

The board elected on Oct 28, 2026 (Jenn Elliot, Jeff Friesen, Shayne Barkman, Jacqueline Morgan, Tess Doerksen, Josh Loewen, Jeremy Neufeld, Erin Williams and Cheryl Froese) took office in November 2026. None of them sat on the board during the pandemic. On this topic it inherits:

  • Policy CB, Communicable Diseases. It was revised in 2020, 2024 and 2025. Jeff Friesen, Shayne Barkman and Cheryl Froese served on the board that voted on it in 2024 and 2025. The posted policy is dated 2024, so whether it reflects the 2025 vote is unclear.
  • No standing pandemic rules. The mask, testing and vaccination requirements were provincial and ended in March 2022. Any future public health orders would again come from the province.
  • Ways of meeting. The board used remote attendance from 2020 to early 2022 and still holds closed e-mail meetings from time to time. HSD streamed one meeting in 2020 but publishes no meeting video.

What we searched, and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Searched: all 1,660 motions, and the full text of every set of minutes and agenda from Mar 2020 to Jun 2022 (and later years for follow-ups), for “COVID”, “pandemic”, “coronavirus”, “remote”, “virtual”, “mask”, “vaccin”, “public health”, “restart”, “social distanc”, “e-mail meeting”, “live stream”, “ventilation” and “communicable”; the roll calls in meetings.csv; Policy CB; HSD’s news posts; Manitoba government releases; and Steinbach Online, CBC, Global, CTV and Winnipeg Free Press coverage.
  • What counts: the 28 decisions are the votes that mention the pandemic or COVID-19, the adoption of minutes of remote, virtual and e-mail meetings held during the pandemic, the 2021 Winter Games remote-learning motion, and every vote on Policy CB from 2020 on. For the religious programs, only final readings and authorizations carrying the pandemic clause are listed; first readings are in Religion in schools.
  • The record’s limits: HSD publishes no meeting video. Minutes are brief, list reports trustees received without their contents, and record vote counts, not who voted which way. Closed (in-camera) and e-mail meeting minutes are not published. Much of what the division did in the pandemic was done by staff under provincial orders and never came to a vote.
  • Couldn’t confirm:
    • Closed meetings: what was decided in the closed meetings of spring 2020, including whether the board voted on the April 2020 EA layoffs. Peters said the board made the call with administration, but no public motion exists. Also unknown: why no public meeting was held in April 2020, and what the e-mail meetings of Dec 18, 2020, Mar 19, 2021 and Sept 2, 2021 were about.
    • Meeting video: whether the June 2020 live stream continued or was kept.
    • Money: what the “COVID Related Expenses” reports showed.
    • Policy CB: what changed in 2020, 2024 and 2025, and why some trustees voted against.
    • Winter Games: whether Niverville’s remote week took place.
    • Case counts: the total number of cases and outbreaks in HSD schools.
    • Figures that disagree: the EA layoff figures differ by source. A Carillon headline in the board’s package says 185 EAs were laid off and 33 kept, while CLAC’s estimate to CBC was about 33 of roughly 220 kept, so this page uses “about 85%”.
    • Sources we could not open: the Carillon’s coverage, including “Trustees ignore vaccination survey” (Oct 21, 2021) and its reports on the SRSS protest; CBC’s 2021 and 2022 stories; and the 2023 Free Press story on a proposed lawsuit, so we don’t know whether it involves HSD.
