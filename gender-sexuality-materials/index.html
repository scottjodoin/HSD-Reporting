Gender, sexuality and classroom materials: what the board decided

What do Hanover schools teach about sexual orientation and gender identity, who decides which books and lessons are allowed, and how much say do parents have? Here is what the board decided from 2013 to 2026, what the province did, and what people on each side said.

Board meetings Jul 2013 – Sept 2026 · 11 decisions in the minutes · Last updated Nov 14, 2026

In short

  • One policy has set the ground rules since 2014. Provincial law requires every board to have a “respect for human diversity” policy. Hanover’s Policy AF was adopted in May 2014 and revised in June 2018. It now says the division follows the province’s guidelines for transgender and gender diverse students.
  • 2016 was the first big public debate. A parent and a student asked the board to let teachers talk about same-sex families in middle-years classrooms. The board kept its guidelines, and two mothers filed a human rights complaint. We could not find out how that complaint ended.
  • In 2024 the board narrowly kept mixed Grade 7 sexual-health classes. A motion to go back to classes split by gender was defeated 4–5. A parent group then asked the education minister to remove six trustees. The minister met both sides and later sent an adviser instead.
  • A new rule for classroom materials took effect in June 2026. Policy AK bans “inappropriate materials,” including sexualized content. It passed all three readings without a vote against (final reading 6–0). The board says it keeps materials age-appropriate; critics call it a book ban.
  • The province overruled the board on “mature minors” in 2026. On Jun 2 the board voted 6–0 that the mature minor category would not be honoured in division buildings. Manitoba law presumes people 16 and older can make their own health-care decisions. After a ministerial directive, the board rescinded the motion 8–1 on Sept 1.
  • Still open: a draft student activism policy, JJ, passed first reading 7–1 and was on the October 2026 agenda. These issues also shaped the October 2026 trustee election.

Background: the rules

Respect for human diversity. Manitoba’s Public Schools Act requires every school board to establish a written policy concerning respect for human diversity and make sure every school follows it. The policy must have “due regard” for the Human Rights Code. It must also accommodate pupils who want to establish and lead clubs that promote respect for “people of all sexual orientations and gender identities,” including clubs called a “gay-straight alliance” (GSA). These rules came from Bill 18, the province’s 2013 anti-bullying law.

Sexual-health classes. The province sets the physical education and health curriculum. Hanover calls the sexual-health parts “sensitive content” (or “sensitive issues”) and teaches them in Grades 5, 7, 9 and 10. Parents are told in advance and can opt their child out. Since June 2026, Policy AK has required two weeks’ written notice before sensitive topics are taught, and has given parents the right to review the materials first.

Health-care decisions. The Health Care Directives Act presumes, unless there is evidence otherwise, that a person who is 16 years of age or more has the capacity to make health care decisions. Health workers call such a young person a “mature minor.” Steinbach Regional Secondary School (SRSS) has had an on-site Southern Health clinic staffed with a full-time nurse practitioner since 2016.

How the board decides. A policy needs three “readings” (votes) before it takes effect. A trustee can give “notice of motion” at one meeting so the board debates the idea at a later one. A great deal of policy work happens in committees and in closed (in-camera) Committee of the Whole meetings. Their minutes are approved in public but not published.

2013–2015: A diversity policy under Bill 18

The board accepts gay-straight alliances

The debate began just before this record starts. In February 2013 a Grade 11 student said he was starting a gay-straight alliance at SRSS. The division told CBC News it had decided a year earlier to accommodate students requesting a Gay Straight Alliance in its high schools. In June 2013, as Bill 18 went through the Legislature, Trustee Lynn Barkman moved that the board ask the minister to change the GSA clause. CBC reported that the board defeated her motion and agreed it will accept and comply with Bill 18.

Policy AF, 2014

The board’s governance committee sent a draft “Respect for Human Diversity” policy to trustees in February 2014. The board gave it first reading on Mar 4, 2014, 6–0 with one trustee abstaining. A revised version went to the full board for final reading on May 6, 2014, 9–0, and the policy lists May 6, 2014 as its creation date. It commits the division to safe, inviting and inclusive learning environments regardless of the grounds in the Human Rights Code, including sexual orientation and gender identity. It also sets the steps students follow to start a club, such as a written proposal to the principal.

In May 2015 the education committee gave trustees a report on “Potentially Sensitive Content” for Grades 5 and 7 and senior years. The board later said the teacher’s guides were updated and approved in 2015. The guides told teachers how to answer students’ questions about sexual orientation.

2016–2019: Same-sex families, a human rights complaint and transgender guidelines

A parent’s request, April 2016

On Apr 5, 2016 a parent spoke to the board about the teaching of sexual orientation and asked it to review Policy AF. The parent, M.M., told CBC News that her child was being bullied for having two mothers. She said she had learned from the superintendent that sexual orientation, abortion and masturbation are not a discussion topic or addressed in any curricular outcomes in Grades 5 to 8. CBC later reported that she received threatening messages online after making the request.

The board explained its position in a statement on Apr 20, 2016. It said sexual orientation was not a learning outcome in the provincial Grade 5 and 7 health curriculum. Teachers who were asked about it could suggest talking after class, point the student to a counsellor or a helpline, or give a short answer about respect. The statement also said “The teacher is also required to inform the student’s parent / guardian” that the child had asked.

“Hanover School Division respects the role of the parents or guardians as the primary educators of their children, especially in the areas pertaining to family life.”

Board of Trustees, official statement · Apr 20, 2016
Outside the minutes

Board chair Ron Falk told CBC News that same-sex families are “just not part of the curriculum” in the middle years, and that many people feel the topic is “maybe best talked about at home.” M.M. said that requiring teachers to tell parents was the real danger: “A policy that requires a teacher to out a student to their parents is dangerous, irresponsible.”

A student’s presentation, and the board’s June response

On May 3, 2016 an SRSS student made the same request. CBC News reported that about 70 people attended and many stood to applaud the student, who asked trustees to change the policies to better include LGBT people. On Jun 7 the minutes say the board “addressed the concerns” raised by the presentations. They do not record what trustees said.

Outside the minutes

CBC News, using a recording made by The Carillon, reported that most trustees spoke against the request. Vice-chair Rick Peters said that taking parents out of the conversation was “going down the path of residential schools.” Trustee Cyndy Friesen, one of two trustees who supported the request, said telling a child to wait until after class was “a violation of laws we are governed by.” Trustee Lynn Barkman, speaking as a health care professional, described sex education she had seen in Toronto and said, “I just feel that there is enough cancer around and the increase in cancer is phenomenal.” She said parents are legally and medically responsible for their children. NDP MLA Wab Kinew raised that comment in the Legislature. Education Minister Ian Wishart said the province was reaching out but was hopeful the issue will be resolved locally.

On Jun 29, 2016 Manitoba Teachers’ Society president Norm Gould presented to the board on creating an inclusive environment and offered the society’s help. He told CBC News the society came to extend that hand of friendship and collaboration.

Human rights complaints

In June 2016 the Public Interest Law Centre filed a complaint with the Manitoba Human Rights Commission on behalf of M.M. and her partner. It alleged that the division was excluding sexual orientation and gender identity from discussion in the classroom. We found no record of a hearing. In October 2018 board chair Ron Falk told The Carillon that “this Human Rights complaint is still before the courts.”

Separate complaints filed in 2017 by three parents, including M.M., were against the provincial government, not the division. They argued that the curriculum left out sexual orientation and gender identity. CBC News reported in 2020 that a commission investigator found enough evidence for a hearing, but the commission’s board voted three members in favour and four against a hearing and dismissed the complaint. The parents asked a court to review that decision.

Provincial transgender guidelines, 2017–2018

In August 2017 the province published Supporting Transgender and Gender Diverse Students in Manitoba Schools. The document appeared in the board’s October 2017 information package beside a Carillon story headlined “Division non-committal on new transgender resource.” The governance committee reviewed it in November. In December the board’s package listed a Carillon story, “Provincial expert leads HSD in transgender document discussion.” On Jun 28, 2018 the board gave the revised Policy AF final reading, 7–0. The current policy says it aligns with provincially recommended supports for transgender students and that “Hanover School Division follows the recommended guidelines.” That September’s package listed a Carillon story, “Hanover to support transgender students.”

The 2018 election

In the 2018 campaign, Trustee Brad Unger, seeking re-election, promised to keep the B.C.-developed SOGI 123 resources (sexual orientation and gender identity) out of Hanover classrooms. CBC News reported his brochure said “you do not need to promote radical LGBT worldviews in order to address bullying.” He told CBC that “Hanover School Division is already inclusive,” with GSAs, a diversity policy and the transgender resource. The Carillon asked all candidates where they stood, and printed their answers.

The board reviewed Policy AF again in June 2019, with no vote recorded. In December 2019 the board’s package listed CBC and Steinbach Online stories about a protest by an LGBT group at Niverville High School. One headline was “HSD Apologizes for trying to silence protest at Niverville High School.” The minutes record no board discussion of it.

2020–2023: A new Grade 7 format, Pride at SRSS and a march in Steinbach

The public minutes for these years contain no votes on this topic. Two changes happened out of public view.

Grade 7 classes stop separating boys and girls

Grade 7 sensitive-issues classes used to be taught with boys and girls in separate rooms. At some point after 2020 the division moved to mixed classes taught in each student’s homeroom. The minutes do not say when or how this was decided. In 2024 Superintendent Shelley Amos told the board that principals had asked for the change “because we have students who are not identifying as biologically male or biologically female, and they need an option.” She said the format’s first year was the previous school year, and that only 41 students out of 574 had opted out.

Pride Month, 2023

In June 2023 rumours spread on social media about a Pride Month event at SRSS. The superintendent told Steinbach Online that the student-led Gay Straight Alliance had suggested students and staff could wear rainbow colours on Jun 7. She added that the day was voluntary and that Policy AF commits the division to support activities that foster respect for human rights. The story was in the board’s June package.

The “Hands Off Our Kids” march, 2023

In the fall of 2023 a “Hands Off Our Kids” march took place in Steinbach. On Oct 3 the board discussed the “Hands Off Our Kids” march. Administration described the safety planning at SRSS and said attendance stayed between 89 and 95 percent. The same meeting received “Standing Together for Safe Schools”, a joint statement from Manitoba’s school boards, superintendents, business officials, parent councils and teachers. The governance committee reviewed Policy AF on Oct 17, 2023, the date of its last review, and shared it with the board in November without a vote.

An HSD statement of Feb 17, 2022 about a protest at SRSS is sometimes linked to this topic. That protest was against COVID-19 mandates; see COVID-19.

2024: Change rooms, a Grade 7 vote and a call to remove trustees

A parent’s request about change rooms

On Apr 2, 2024 a parent spoke to the board about the change rooms at SRSS and students “feeling safe.” The parent asked the board to review the policy and offer “three bathrooms to accommodate all.” Steinbach Online did not name the parent, to protect the students involved, and neither do we. The parent said his son had been uncomfortable when a student who identifies as a boy changed in the boys’ room. He called separate rooms “a reasonable compromise” and said “It’s not about making the other kid feel unvalued.” Board chair Brad Unger said it “takes courage to come before the board” and promised a reply.

The Grade 7 motion is defeated 4–5

At the same meeting Trustee Shayne Barkman, seconded by Trustee Dallas Wiebe, moved the motion he had given notice of in March: “that HSD teaches the Grade 7 sensitive issues the same way it was taught prior to school year 2020-2021.” That would have meant separate classes for boys and girls again. It was defeated 4–5. According to Steinbach Online, Trustees Shayne Barkman, Lynn Barkman, Jeff Friesen and Dallas Wiebe voted in favour, and Ron Falk, Charmaine Toews, Danielle Funk, Cheryl Froese and Brad Unger voted against.

  • For: Trustee Shayne Barkman said parents he met while campaigning preferred the old way, which was “more respectful to all students who attended as well as to all teachers.” Trustee Dallas Wiebe said the families who opted out still mattered: in trying to include everyone, “you have to pick and choose whose rights are going to be honoured.”
  • Against: Trustee Charmaine Toews said the motion would “undo all the work we did as a board” and that a late change would stress schools. Trustee Danielle Funk said the board had chosen the format as “the most respectful for the most amount of students,” and that reversing it after one year was “just ludicrous.”

The same evening, a motion to have trustees take part in hiring music and gym teachers carried 5–4. That decision is covered in Board governance.

A parent group asks the minister to remove six trustees

On Apr 25, 2024 the Hanover Parent Alliance for Diversity (HPAD), whose members do not give their names, wrote to Education Minister Nello Altomare. It asked him to remove six trustees from the Hanover School Board: Brad Unger, Jeff Friesen, Shayne Barkman, Lynn Barkman, Cheryl Froese and Dallas Wiebe. It also asked for a provincial overseer and a review of board policies. The letter said the delegation should not have been allowed to speak, that the hiring and Grade 7 motions were discriminatory, and that two trustees had attended the 2023 march. These were allegations; the minister did not remove any trustee. The group said its petition had nearly 900 signatures. A Mitchell parent, N.W., started a counter-petition supporting the trustees, which The Carillon reported had more than 4,000 signatures. Its text said the trustees “consistently make policy decisions that reflect the ideologies of the local residents.”

Board chair Brad Unger told The Carillon the board stood by letting people speak as delegations: “We stand by our policies and procedures for welcoming that communication from our community members.” After meeting the minister, the board issued a statement on May 3:

“Hanover School Division will continue to fully adhere to the Manitoba curriculum and Provincial legislation. The Board is committed to providing all students with a safe, inclusive, and respectful environment, as outlined in our Respect for Human Diversity Policy.”

Board of Trustees, response to the Parent Alliance for Diversity · May 3, 2024

The statement also said the board would welcome a meeting with the Parent Alliance for Diversity.

The board’s answer, May 7, 2024

About 80 people came to the May 7 meeting. The minutes say the board discussed the rationale for the current Respect for Human Diversity Policy with reference to the Public Schools Act. Steinbach Online reported that the board denied the request for separate rooms. Unger listed what SRSS already has: separate change rooms for boys and girls with private stalls, gender-neutral washrooms, and single-stall washrooms. “Any student who is uncomfortable in the public change area for any reason can use a private stall or individual washroom.” He said the division is legally obligated to follow the Act, the Human Rights Code and the Charter. He named three points of common ground, including “every kid should feel safe at school.”

Outside the minutes

Reactions differed. The Carillon quoted a parent who felt the board “gladhanded and spoke niceties” and had not addressed marginalized students’ experiences. HPAD told Steinbach Online that Unger had said the diversity policy “is only there out of ‘obligation’”, which the group called “the bare minimum.” Unger said in a statement that the board “had a productive discussion with the Education Minister.”

Minister Altomare said he would send help, such as “a retired superintendent, maybe someone from the legal community.” Asked about removing trustees, he said “we’ll have to wait until the next round of elections.” In August 2024 the province appointed former superintendent Kelly Barkman as adviser to the board. His role is covered in Board governance.

In June 2024 the board received a Manitoba Association of School Superintendents release on “A Guide for Student Belonging in Public Schools in Manitoba.” On Jun 27 its agenda included a “Library Materials Inquiry” from the governance committee. The minutes give no details and record no vote.

2025–2026: Classroom materials, mature minors and student activism

Policy AK: what counts as an “inappropriate” resource

A draft of Policy AK, Responsible Use of Educational Resources, reached the board in November 2025. On Dec 2, 2025 it passed first reading 7–0. The minutes note requests to address who would sit on a review committee and how the existing book selection procedure was built into the policy, and a question whether it “could address other moral issues.” It passed second reading 8–0 on May 5, 2026 and third and final reading 6–0 on Jun 2, with three trustees absent. Trustee Jeff Friesen moved all three readings.

The policy, now in effect, bans “Inappropriate Materials” from being bought, displayed or used in teaching. The definition includes material with graphic or explicit content, graphic depictions of extreme physical harm, or vulgar language. It also covers “Sexualized Content,” meaning material that encourages sexual activity or places “primary emphasis on sexualized desire rather than legitimate educational objectives such as human health.” The policy says it “is not intended to restrict legitimate educational inquiry,” and that the division must still deliver the provincial curriculum. Principals enforce it. A parent’s appeal to the board is heard in a closed meeting. The policy says no one faces reprisal for a good-faith concern, and that staff who do not comply may be disciplined.

Outside the minutes

The board’s case. Board chair Dallas Wiebe told The Carillon the policy had been in the works for about two years after complaints from parents, students and teachers about graphic and inappropriate materials, “and not by a small margin.” He declined to give examples. He said principals are well placed to decide: “I have confidence in our principals.”

The critics’ case. An HPAD spokesperson called the policy “essentially a book ban.” Manitoba Teachers’ Society president Lillian Klausen called it “a troubling message of the kind of school environment” the division was creating. Neil McArthur of the University of Manitoba’s Centre for Professional and Applied Ethics said it “puts the power in the hands of the small minority who are willing to complain.” Education Minister Tracy Schmidt met the trustees about the policy. The Carillon reported that she said the board would put procedures in place so marginalized students are not targeted.

The “mature minor” motion

Late in the Jun 2, 2026 meeting, Trustee Cheryl Froese, seconded by Trustee Shayne Barkman, moved “That HSD parents / guardians be regarded as primary healthcare provider for their child. As such, the ‘mature minor’ category is not to be honored in any HSD building.” The six trustees present carried it 6–0. CBC reported that the wording was not in the agenda and became public only when the minutes were published. CBC also reported that the motion was a directive to staff to create a draft policy, so it had not yet been put into practice.

  • For: Froese told Steinbach Online that it “is absurd” to say a minor can make health decisions without a parent, and that staff would still report children at risk. Wiebe said parents have “the right and the responsibility” to be involved, and that the motion followed parent delegations. In an email to CBC he said the board “has heard concerns from parents regarding trust, communication, and transparency.”
  • Against: Sherry Gott of the Manitoba Advocate for Children and Youth said the motion “violates the rights of young people” under the UN Convention on the Rights of the Child and Manitoba law. The Manitoba Human Rights Commission encouraged the board to review its obligations. HPAD said students “may now question if they can trust” professionals. Southern Health, which runs the SRSS clinic offering reproductive and mental health care, said it was gathering additional information.

Schmidt met all trustees on Jun 16 and said “The law is that mature minors can provide consent for their health care.” On Jun 23 a delegation presented concerns about the motion. CBC reported that the parent, J.C., brought a petition with 1,000 signatures and asked trustees to consult health professionals. Vice-chair Jeff Friesen said the petition should have used the motion’s exact wording. CBC reported that Wiebe said the board would keep working on the policy. Of parents, he said “They love their children, and they would like to be involved.”

The province steps in

On Jul 22 Schmidt issued a directive under the Public Schools Act requiring the board to rescind the motion. CBC News reported that her letter said the motion conflicted with the Health Care Directives Act, the Public Health Act and the Charter. It also said “a reasonable observer” would conclude the board acted “at best for public attention and at worst to pick a fight.” Schmidt said the “nuclear” option of dissolving the board and appointing a trustee was possible but that she hoped it would not come to that.

La Verendrye MLA Konrad Narth (Progressive Conservative) said the province respects local autonomy “only when it aligns with the ideology of the government.” He also said a board’s bylaws “cannot overrun provincial law.” In an Aug 7 letter, Wiebe told the minister the motion had followed “substantial engagement with Hanover families” and asked how mature-minor decisions are made in schools.

On Sept 1, 2026, after receiving the minister’s letter, the board voted 8–1 to rescind the motion. Froese moved it and Lynn Barkman seconded. CBC News reported that Vice-chair Jeff Friesen was the lone dissenting vote. In the debate, Trustee Ron Falk said the intent was valid but the board should “stay in education circles.” Trustee Danielle Funk said she did not think “the rights of our students come underneath the rights of the parent.” Friesen said the board was concerned about “transparency and parental trust.”

A student activism policy

The governance committee discussed “Activism in Schools Policy Development” in June 2025. On May 5, 2026 the board voted 8–0, on Trustee Brad Unger’s motion as amended, “That the Board develop a policy that addresses activism in our schools.” The new policy would decide “what, if any, kind of activism is appropriate.” Unger said “Students can be easily influenced” by trusted adults. Friesen said activism by schools “erodes the relationship” with families. Falk, who proposed changing “regulates” to “addresses,” noted “a lot of good activism that takes place.” A provincial spokesperson told The Carillon that the provincial code of conduct does not consider activism an inappropriate student behaviour unless it involves something like violence, harassment or discrimination.

On Jun 23, 2026 Policy JJ passed first reading 7–1. The draft supports “age-appropriate student activism” on causes such as the environment, social justice and inclusion. It bars activities that promote hate or disrupt learning, and it requires staff supervision and the principal’s awareness or approval. It says school resources “shall not be used to endorse political parties or candidates.” Trustee Funk asked who decides when a complaint is raised, because “activism is political, no matter how neutral people try to be.”

The 2026 election

These debates carried into the October 2026 election. Trustee Brad Unger, who was running for Steinbach city council, sent trustee candidates a seven-question email on sex and gender, pronouns, classroom flags, race and parents. Vice-chair Jeff Friesen posted answers that he said were “consistent with Scripture” and with biology. TransManitoba’s executive director, Charlie Eau, responded that “nobody is harmed by the presence of a rainbow flag.” CBC reported that, according to Manitoba Teachers’ Society president Lillian Klausen, the society sent a cease and desist letter over an AI-generated image shared on Trustee Shayne Barkman’s campaign page, and that he took it down. Questions about trustee conduct are covered in Board governance.

After the election CBC reported that three newly elected Steinbach trustees ran against the emphasis of the previous board, while the other six had served on it or backed its policies. New chair Jeff Friesen said the policies did not target any group: “Show me one policy where you start naming people groups.”

What the new board inherits

The board elected on Oct 28, 2026 (Jenn Elliot, Jeff Friesen, Shayne Barkman, Jacqueline Morgan, Tess Doerksen, Josh Loewen, Jeremy Neufeld, Erin Williams and Cheryl Froese) took office in November 2026. It chose Jeff Friesen as chair and Shayne Barkman as vice-chair. On this topic, the record shows:

  • Policy JJ (student activism) has had only first reading. An updated draft was on the Oct 6, 2026 agenda. The minutes of that meeting were not yet published when this page was written, so we don’t know what happened to it.
  • Policy AK (educational resources) is in effect. It requires principals to review it with staff each fall and the superintendent to report to the board periodically on reviews done under it. No such report has appeared in the public minutes yet.
  • The mature minor question is closed as a motion. In its Aug 7 letter, though, the board told the minister that families’ concerns would be included in “any policy drafted to ensure healthcare continues at the SRSS.” It also asked the province to explain how mature-minor decisions are made in schools.
  • Policy AF (respect for human diversity) was last reviewed in October 2023. It still commits the division to the provincial transgender guidelines and to accommodating student clubs such as GSAs.

About this page

Built from the board’s published minutes (Jul 2013 – Nov 2026), agendas, policies and news releases on hsd.ca, Manitoba statutes, and news coverage linked in the text. See How these reports are made and Data accuracy. Religious exercises and the 2026 debate over the province’s anti-Islamophobia plan are covered in Religion in schools. Trustee conduct, the 2024 hiring motion and the provincial adviser are covered in Board governance.

What we searched, and what we couldn’t confirm
  • Searched: every motion, minutes page and agenda from Jul 2013 to Nov 2026 for “sexual”, “gender”, “diversity”, “sensitive”, “LGBT”, “2SLGBTQ”, “Pride”, “GSA”, “transgender”, “library”, “book”, “resources”, “Parent Alliance”, “activism”, “mature minor”, “protest”, “washroom” and “delegation”; Policies AF, AK and JE; HSD news posts; and coverage by CBC, Steinbach Online, The Carillon, Global News and others.
  • What counts: the 11 decisions are votes on Policies AF and AK, the Grade 7 motion, the mature minor motion and its repeal, and the activism motion and Policy JJ. The Apr 2, 2024 hiring motion and the trustee-conduct matters are left out, because they belong to governance.
  • Checked and not about this topic: HSD’s Feb 17, 2022 statement was about an anti-mandate (COVID-19) protest at SRSS, during which a Canada flag was stolen from the flagpole. The “delegations” items on Oct 3 and Nov 7, 2023 were a school business officials’ memo on board delegation procedures. The Dec 2, 2025 Policy JE (Graduation) vote, 4–3, gave schools more say over ceremony dates and banquets, according to Steinbach Online; see Learning programs.
  • The record’s limits: HSD publishes no meeting video. The minutes are brief and record vote counts, not who voted which way. Closed (in-camera) meeting minutes are not published, and committee reports are listed by title only. Individual votes named on this page come from news reports, as marked. People who spoke to the board as members of the public are identified only by role or initials, and students are not named.
  • Couldn’t confirm: how the 2016 human rights complaint against the division ended, and how the court review of the 2017 complaints against the province ended. We also couldn’t confirm when and how the Grade 7 format changed (the motion says 2020/21; the superintendent described one year of use by spring 2024), or what the board said to the student in 2016. Other gaps: what the June 2024 “Library Materials Inquiry” was; which materials led to Policy AK; how the book selection procedure was folded into it; who voted against Policy JJ; and what happened to JJ in October 2026. The Carillon’s 2017 and 2018 stories on the transgender guidelines and the 2019 Niverville stories are known here only from headlines in the board’s packages; we did not open them.
